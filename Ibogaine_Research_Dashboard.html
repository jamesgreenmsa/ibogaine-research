Veterans most likely to seek ibogaine treatment are already taking a medication that makes it dangerous.

We studied more than 600,000 people with opioid use disorder across three independent databases. In Medicare, nearly 30 percent of those most likely to be considered for ibogaine were actively taking a medication that ibogaine trials require patients to stop before treatment — often a psychiatric medication they depend on for daily stability.

The same pattern, confirmed across three cohorts

We replicated the analysis in three separate datasets — a national research cohort, a regional hospital system, and Medicare fee-for-service. The prevalence varies by cohort, but the direction is consistent: people most likely to seek ibogaine carry restricted medications at roughly 4 to 8 times the rate of those who are not.

The clinical problem

Screening identifies the risk. It does not resolve it.

Existing ibogaine protocols handle a positive medication screen in one of two ways: exclude the patient from treatment, or require them to stop the medication before treatment begins. Neither approach answers the harder question — what should a clinician do when a patient's psychiatric stability depends on that medication? Stopping a long-term antidepressant carries its own risks: withdrawal, relapse, hospitalization. These are two competing risks with no current evidence base to guide the decision.

The Screen-to-Action Gap
Should the medication be tapered? Switched to a different drug? How long should a washout last? When should ibogaine treatment be delayed or deferred altogether? The evidence needed to support those decisions does not yet exist for the psychedelic therapy context. This is the research gap our program is designed to close.

Background
01 What is ibogaine and why does it matter now?

Ibogaine is a plant-derived compound with a long history of ceremonial use that has attracted serious scientific interest as a potential treatment for opioid use disorder. Early clinical studies have shown dramatic reductions in withdrawal symptoms and cravings — in some cases, lasting effects from a single dose.

In April 2026, a U.S. executive order directed federal agencies to develop pathways for expanded ibogaine access, specifically for veterans with treatment-refractory opioid use disorder. This brought the safety question from research interest to urgent policy reality.

The challenge: ibogaine carries a known cardiac risk, particularly in patients who are already taking certain psychiatric medications. Understanding how common that overlap is — and what to do about it — is the core of our research program.
Ibogaine is a plant-derived compound showing real promise for opioid use disorder. A 2026 executive order directed expanded access, especially for veterans. But ibogaine carries cardiac risk that interacts with common psychiatric medications. Our research asks: how widespread is that overlap, and what should clinicians do about it?
02 Why these medications create a problem

Ibogaine is processed — cleared from the body — by a specific enzyme. Some common psychiatric medications slow that enzyme down. When the enzyme is inhibited, ibogaine stays in the body longer, builds up to higher levels, and its cardiac effects are amplified.

The medications that cause this interaction are some of the most commonly prescribed drugs for depression, anxiety, and PTSD — the exact conditions most prevalent in the people with opioid use disorder most likely to be considered for ibogaine treatment.

  • Paroxetine (Paxil) — among the strongest inhibitors, widely prescribed for PTSD and depression
  • Fluoxetine (Prozac) — long half-life makes even a completed taper risky for weeks
  • Other antidepressants, antipsychotics, and QT-prolonging agents with cardiac interaction potential
Our analysis of FDA adverse-event reports has already identified a cardiac safety signal in patients who received ibogaine. This is not a theoretical risk — it is already appearing in real-world data.
Ibogaine is cleared by a specific enzyme. Certain psychiatric medications — paroxetine, fluoxetine, and others — slow that enzyme down. When these drugs are combined with ibogaine, drug levels rise and cardiac risk increases. We have already identified this as a cardiac safety signal in FDA adverse-event data. The problem is that these are exactly the medications most commonly prescribed for PTSD and depression — the conditions most prevalent in the OUD population most likely to seek ibogaine treatment.
The Data Sources
03 Three independent databases — why we used all three
NIH All of Us

A national NIH research program enrolling over 750,000 participants, with linked electronic health records, genetic data, and self-reported survey information. We used All of Us to identify patients with opioid use disorder and to characterize their medication and genetic profiles. A key strength: All of Us includes genotype data confirming which patients carry the enzyme variant that makes ibogaine interactions most dangerous.

Rutgers CRDW

The Rutgers Clinical Research Data Warehouse — a regional hospital system database covering New Jersey. We used this to replicate the analysis in a real-world clinical population, with adjusted estimates controlling for age and sex.

Medicare FFS

Medicare fee-for-service claims data covering more than 590,000 people with opioid use disorder. Medicare gives us the largest, most representative picture of the U.S. OUD population, including actual medication fill records showing which prescriptions were active at any given point in time.

Using three independent databases is a form of replication. If the finding is real, it should appear in all three — and it did. The absolute numbers differ by cohort, but the direction is consistent every time.
We used three independent databases. NIH All of Us gives us genetic data and self-reported surveys for a diverse national cohort. Rutgers CRDW gives us a real-world regional clinical population. Medicare FFS gives us the largest representative picture of the U.S. OUD population with actual prescription fill records. The point of using all three is replication: if the finding is real, it appears in every database. It did.
The Analysis
04 How we defined ibogaine candidacy

Ibogaine is not approved in the U.S. and has not been widely administered in formal clinical settings, which means our databases do not contain patients who actually received it. Instead, we defined a proxy population of likely candidates using criteria drawn from published ibogaine trial protocols.

Ibogaine candidates were defined as patients with opioid use disorder who also had a diagnosis of PTSD, depression, or anxiety — the psychiatric comorbidities most commonly cited as motivating ibogaine treatment, and the conditions most frequently targeted by the restricted medications we were studying.

Non-candidates were patients with opioid use disorder who did not have those psychiatric diagnoses. The comparison between these two groups is the core of the analysis.

This is a prevalence study, not a causal study. We are not claiming that these patients will have a cardiac event. We are documenting how many people in the most likely candidate population are already on medications that trial protocols would require them to stop — and what that means for the scale of the clinical problem.
Because ibogaine is not widely administered in the U.S., we defined a proxy candidate population: patients with OUD who also had PTSD, depression, or anxiety. Non-candidates had OUD without those psychiatric diagnoses. We compared restricted medication prevalence between the two groups. This is a prevalence study — we are documenting the scale of the clinical problem, not predicting individual outcomes.
Key Findings
05 What we found — the headline numbers

Across all three databases, ibogaine candidates were far more likely to be actively taking a restricted medication than non-candidates.

  • NIH All of Us: 45.5% of candidates vs. 5.5% of non-candidates — a fold difference of 8.3 times, unadjusted OR 14.39
  • Rutgers CRDW: 24.0% of candidates vs. 3.5% of non-candidates — a fold difference of 6.9 times, adjusted OR 8.31
  • Medicare FFS: 15.8% of candidates vs. 4.1% of non-candidates — a fold difference of 3.9 times, adjusted PR 3.51

The composite restriction burden in Medicare — the percentage of candidates carrying at least one medication from any restricted class — was 29.5 percent. That is nearly 1 in 3 candidates actively taking a medication that ibogaine protocols would require them to stop before treatment.

The genotype finding from All of Us is striking: 81.7% of ibogaine candidates carried a CYP2D6 inhibitor genotype, compared to 82.2% of non-candidates — a statistically non-significant difference. The risk is driven by what patients are prescribed, not by their genetics alone.
The headline finding: across all three databases, ibogaine candidates carry restricted medications at 4 to 8 times the rate of non-candidates. In Medicare, nearly 30 percent of candidates — almost 1 in 3 — had an active restricted medication. The genotype finding is equally important: genetic risk alone does not explain the difference. Prescription patterns do. The people most likely to seek ibogaine treatment are the same people most commonly prescribed the medications that make it dangerous.
The Clinical Problem
06 The screen-to-action gap

Existing ibogaine trials and treatment protocols handle this problem in one of two ways: they exclude patients on restricted medications, or they require those medications to be stopped before treatment begins. What they do not provide is any guidance on what to do when a patient depends on one of those medications for their psychiatric stability.

This is what we call the screen-to-action gap. Pre-treatment screening correctly identifies the medication flag. But the evidence needed to act on it — how to taper safely, how long a washout should last, when to defer treatment, when the cardiac risk is low enough to proceed — does not yet exist in the psychedelic therapy context.

The clinical decision a patient faces when they test positive on a medication screen is a decision between two competing risks: the cardiac risk of proceeding toward ibogaine with an enzyme-inhibiting medication still active, versus the psychiatric risk of stopping a medication they may have depended on for years.
Existing protocols exclude patients on restricted medications or require discontinuation. Neither approach tells the clinician what to do when the patient needs that medication to stay psychiatrically stable. We call this the screen-to-action gap. It is a decision between two competing risks: cardiac risk from the drug interaction on one side, psychiatric risk from stopping a long-term medication on the other. No evidence-based guidance currently exists for how to navigate that decision in the ibogaine context.
What We Are Asking For
07 Our recommendations — Document, Track, Connect

We presented oral comment to the FDA on September 14, 2026, asking for three things — summarized as DTC: Document, Track, Connect.

  • Document — require early-phase ibogaine trials to record the actual reason each patient is excluded, not just that they were excluded. Those reasons become analyzable evidence that can teach future clinicians what patterns of risk led to exclusion decisions.
  • Track — require reporting of what clinicians actually decided to do when a patient tested positive on a medication screen. Did they taper? Switch medications? Defer treatment? That decision log is the beginning of a real evidence base.
  • Connect — use FDA's own adverse-event reporting system alongside the Sentinel real-world data infrastructure to investigate which medication management decisions, after a positive screen, led to better or worse outcomes for patients.
Screening tells us who may be at risk. The next evidence standard from FDA must tell clinicians what to do with a positive screen.
We asked FDA for three things: Document why patients are excluded from early trials so those decisions become analyzable evidence. And Connect FDA's adverse-event system to the Sentinel real-world data infrastructure to study which management decisions lead to better outcomes. Screening tells us who may be at risk. 08 Where the research goes next

Paper 1 — the systematic review and FDA adverse-event analysis establishing the cardiac safety signal — has been submitted to the Journal of Opioid Management and is currently under review.

Paper 2 — this three-cohort prevalence analysis — is being prepared for submission to JAMA Network Open.

Paper 3, now in design, will build a tiered clinical decision framework for managing the competing risks a clinician faces when a patient screens positive. The framework will draw on three sources:

  • Medicare claims data to characterize medication dependence patterns and psychiatric stability proxies at population scale
  • NIH All of Us survey data — including the validated PHQ-9 depression screener and GAD-7 anxiety screener — to add the psychiatric severity context that claims data cannot capture
  • Published antidepressant discontinuation literature on paroxetine and fluoxetine to calibrate the psychiatric risk side of the decision
The research program is being presented at AMSUS 2027 and Psychedelic Science 2027, both in Denver in May 2027. A written comment has been submitted to FDA Docket 2026-14155.
